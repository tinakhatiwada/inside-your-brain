Neural Communication

How neurons talk to each other through electrical and chemical signals

Every thought, sensation, and action depends on neurons communicating through a combination of electrical impulses and chemical messengers.

The Action Potential

Neurons communicate via action potentials — brief electrical pulses that travel along the axon at up to 400 mph. This works through an all-or-nothing principle: when a neuron receives enough stimulation, voltage-gated sodium channels open in rapid succession down the axon, creating a wave of electrical depolarization. Myelin sheaths (produced by glial cells) insulate the axon and allow the signal to "jump" between nodes, dramatically increasing speed.

Synaptic Transmission

When the action potential reaches the axon terminal, it triggers the release of neurotransmitters — chemical molecules that cross the synaptic cleft (a gap of ~20 nanometers) and bind to receptors on the next neuron. Key neurotransmitters include:

  • Glutamate — The brain's main excitatory neurotransmitter, involved in learning and memory
  • GABA — The main inhibitory neurotransmitter, preventing over-excitation (seizures)
  • Dopamine — Reward, motivation, and motor control. Implicated in addiction and Parkinson's
  • Serotonin — Mood regulation, sleep, and appetite. Target of most antidepressants
  • Acetylcholine — Memory, attention, and muscle activation. Lost in Alzheimer's disease

Neural Circuits

Individual synaptic connections are meaningless in isolation — it's the patterns of connectivity that matter. Neurons organize into circuits where the strength of connections can be modified by experience (synaptic plasticity). This is the biological basis of learning: "neurons that fire together wire together" (Hebb's Rule).

Sensory Perception

How your brain transforms raw physical stimuli into conscious experience

Perception is far more than passive recording. Your brain is a prediction machine that constructs reality from incomplete sensory data, filling in gaps with expectations and prior experience.

Vision — The Dominant Sense

Vision consumes roughly 30% of your cortex. Light entering the eye activates rod cells (low-light, black/white) and cone cells (color, detail) in the retina. These signals travel via the optic nerve to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, then to V1 in the occipital lobe, where features like edges, orientation, and color are extracted.

Audition — Sound Processing

Sound waves vibrate the eardrum, which transmits through the ossicles to the cochlea. Hair cells in the cochlea convert vibrations into electrical signals sorted by frequency (tonotopic mapping). The auditory cortex in the temporal lobe assembles these frequencies into meaningful sounds.

Cross-Modal Integration

Senses don't work in isolation. The superior temporal sulcus and parietal association areas combine visual, auditory, and tactile information into unified perceptions. This is why seeing lips move helps you hear speech (the McGurk effect), and why food "tastes" different with a stuffy nose.

Memory & Learning

How the brain encodes, stores, and retrieves information across a lifetime

Memory isn't a single system — it's a collection of interacting processes distributed across the brain.

Types of Memory

  • Sensory Memory — Ultra-brief (milliseconds to seconds) trace of raw sensory input
  • Working Memory — The brain's "mental workspace" (prefrontal cortex), holding 4±1 items for seconds to minutes
  • Long-term Memory — Potentially permanent storage, divided into explicit (declarative) and implicit (procedural) types

Consolidation

The hippocampus is critical for converting short-term experiences into long-term memories through consolidation. During sleep (especially slow-wave sleep), the hippocampus "replays" the day's experiences, strengthening important neural pathways and pruning irrelevant ones. This is why sleep deprivation devastates learning.

Neuroplasticity

The brain physically changes with every new experience. Long-term potentiation (LTP) strengthens synaptic connections between frequently co-activated neurons. Over time, repeated practice can increase gray matter density (as seen in London taxi drivers' enlarged hippocampi) or create entirely new neural pathways.

Emotions & Cognition

How emotions shape thinking, memory, and decision-making

Emotions aren't obstacles to clear thinking — they are integral to intelligent behavior. The brain systems for emotion and cognition are deeply interconnected.

The Limbic System

The amygdala evaluates the emotional significance of stimuli, particularly threats. The hypothalamus translates emotional states into bodily responses (stress hormones, heart rate changes). The cingulate cortex bridges emotion and cognition.

Emotional Memory

Emotionally charged events are remembered more vividly and durably. The amygdala modulates hippocampal memory formation — essentially "tagging" important experiences for preferential storage. This is adaptive but can become maladaptive in PTSD, where traumatic memories are too vivid and intrusive.

Emotion and Decision-Making

The ventromedial prefrontal cortex integrates emotional "gut feelings" (somatic markers) with rational analysis. Without this integration, people with frontal lobe damage make logically sound arguments but catastrophically poor real-world decisions, because they can't feel the weight of consequences.

Reasoning & Behavior

Executive functions, cognitive control, and how the brain produces purposeful behavior

The ability to think abstractly, plan for the future, and override impulses is centered in the prefrontal cortex — the most evolutionarily recent part of the human brain.

Executive Functions

  • Working Memory — Holding and manipulating information (dorsolateral PFC)
  • Cognitive Flexibility — Switching between tasks and mental frameworks
  • Inhibitory Control — Suppressing inappropriate impulses and distractions
  • Planning — Sequencing steps to achieve a goal
  • Monitoring — Evaluating performance and adjusting strategies

The Default Mode Network

When not focused on external tasks, the brain enters a default mode — a network of brain regions (medial PFC, posterior cingulate, angular gyrus) that activates during daydreaming, self-reflection, mental time travel, and imagining others' perspectives.

Cognitive Biases

The brain uses mental shortcuts (heuristics) to make quick decisions. While often useful, these can lead to systematic errors: confirmation bias, anchoring, and availability heuristic.

Brain Network Cooperation

How distributed neural networks collaborate to produce complex mental processes

No brain region works alone. Complex cognition emerges from the dynamic interaction of large-scale neural networks.

Major Brain Networks

  • Central Executive Network — Focused attention and working memory (PFC + parietal)
  • Default Mode Network — Self-reflection, imagination, social cognition
  • Salience Network — Detecting important stimuli, switching between other networks
  • Sensorimotor Network — Coordinating perception with action
  • Visual Network — Processing and interpreting visual information

Dynamic Connectivity

The brain constantly reconfigures which networks are active and how they interact. The salience network acts as a "switch operator," toggling between the externally-focused executive network and the internally-focused default mode network. Dysfunction in this switching mechanism is implicated in ADHD and depression.

Emergent Consciousness

Consciousness itself may arise from the global integration of information across distributed brain networks — the Global Workspace Theory proposes that conscious awareness occurs when information is "broadcast" widely across the cortex, making it available to multiple cognitive processes simultaneously.
